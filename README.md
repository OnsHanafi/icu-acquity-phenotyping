Intensive care units generate large volumes of structured clinical data, including vital signs, laboratory measurements, admission information, and patient demographics. These data can be used not only for outcome prediction, but also to identify clinically meaningful groups of patients who share similar physiological and laboratory patterns. This project used the MIMIC-IV Clinical Database Demo to perform unsupervised patient grouping among ICU patients. The main objective was to explore whether early ICU information from the first 24 hours could be used to define patient phenotypes that differ in acuity, as measured using hospital mortality and ICU length of stay. The final analytic cohort included 100 patients, with the first ICU stay selected for each patient to avoid repeated patient-level observations. Features were extracted from demographic, admission, ICU stay, chart event, and laboratory event tables. Vital sign variables included heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, summarized using median, minimum, and maximum values from the first 24 hours of ICU admission. Laboratory variables included pH, electrolytes, renal markers, hematology markers, glucose, and anion gap, summarized using median and maximum values. After cleaning and feature engineering, missing values were handled using median imputation for numerical variables and most-frequent imputation for categorical variables. Numerical variables were standardized, categorical variables were one-hot encoded, and principal component analysis was used for two-dimensional visualization.

 Two clustering approaches were compared: K-Means clustering and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM). K-Means was selected with four clusters based on the elbow method, while GMM was selected with five components based on AIC/BIC exploration. Model quality was evaluated using internal clustering metrics, phenotype acuity profiles, and statistical validation. K-Means performed better on geometric clustering metrics, with a higher silhouette score, lower Davies-Bouldin index, and higher Calinski-Harabasz score than GMM. However, both models showed weak-to-moderate internal separation, suggesting that ICU patient phenotypes overlap in high-dimensional clinical space. In contrast, GMM produced more clinically meaningful phenotypes. The highest-mortality GMM phenotype reached 60.0% mortality, compared with 31.6% in the highest-mortality K-Means phenotype. GMM also showed stronger statistical separation, with 41 of 43 tested variables significant at p < 0.05 compared with 37 for K-Means. Overall, the results suggest that probabilistic clustering may better capture clinically meaningful ICU acuity patterns even when internal clustering metrics are modest. The study demonstrates how unsupervised learning can support ICU patient stratification, while also highlighting limitations related to small sample size, class imbalance, outliers, correlated features, and the exploratory nature of clustering.
